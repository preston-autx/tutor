LESSON 1 · ESSENCE OF CALCULUS

The
Circle Mystery

You've written A = πr² a hundred times. But why is it true? Today we don't memorize it — we discover it, and calculus is born in our hands.

Start the story ↓
THE HOOK

A formula you trust but can't explain

Quick — where does πr² come from? Not "the teacher said so." The actual reason. Most people who use this formula every day have never seen why it's true. By the end of this page, you will — and you'll have invented a piece of calculus to get there.

1

Cut the circle into rings

Think of a tree trunk, or an onion. A disk is just a stack of thin rings nested inside each other — from a tiny one in the center out to the edge. Understand one ring, understand the whole circle.

2

Unroll one ring → it's basically a rectangle

Take the ring at radius r with a tiny thickness dr. Snip it and straighten it out. Its length is the circumference 2πr; its height is dr. So its area is about:

area of one ring ≈ 2πr · dr
3

Line up every unrolled ring

Each ring becomes a thin bar. The center ring is tiny (length ≈ 0); the outer ring is the longest (2πR). Stand them side by side, shortest to longest, and a shape appears. Go play 👇

THE PUNCHLINE

The bars are a triangle

As N→∞, the staircase becomes the straight line y = 2πr, and the bars fill a perfect right triangle. Its area is dead simple:

A = ½ · base · height = ½ · R · 2πR = πR²

That's it. The mysterious πr² is just the area of a triangle in disguise. And the move we made — chopping a shape into infinitely many tiny pieces and adding them up — has a name: integration. You just did your first integral:

∫0R 2πr dr = πR²

"Area under the line 2πr" and "area of the circle" turned out to be the same number. That bridge — between adding up tiny slices and area under a curve — is the heartbeat of all of calculus.
